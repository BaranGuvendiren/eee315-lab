1.a) Each value is useful in a different situation:
Vp is used when the maximum voltage is important. Examples are checking that the diode’s reverse voltage rating is not exceeded, or finding the maximum output of a rectifier or clipper. 
If the waveform is not symmetric around ground, the positive and negative peaks should be measured separately.
Vpp is used when we want the total swing of the signal. It does not depend on the DC offset or the ground reference. 
For example, we set the function generator as 10 Vpp, and Vpp is also used for ripple or noise amplitude.
Vrms is used when power or heating is important, since P = Vrms²/R holds for any waveform. It is also the best value for comparing waveforms with different shapes.

1.b) For power analysis, RMS is the right choice. If the total power is needed, the DC part must be included (AC+DC RMS).
If only the ripple is of interest, the AC RMS is enough. For amplitude comparison, Vpp or Vp is easier because it can be read directly from the oscilloscope screen, but this only works well if the waveforms have the same shape.
For waveform characterization, one value is not enough. We would measure the peaks, Vpp, the DC value, the RMS value, and the timing (period, duty cycle, turn-on/turn-off times). The crest factor and ripple factor can then be calculated from these.

1.c) The three tools work differently:
Oscilloscope: It samples the signal with an ADC. It finds Vmax, Vmin and Vpp from the samples and calculates RMS numerically as √(Σv²/N). Because it uses the actual samples, its RMS is correct for any waveform shape.
The “cycle RMS” option, which uses whole periods, is the most accurate.
Multimeter: Cheaper meters are average-responding. They rectify the signal, take the average, and multiply by 1.11, so they are only correct for sine waves. True-RMS meters calculate the real RMS but have limited bandwidth and crest factor. 
In AC mode they block the DC part. Multimeters usually cannot measure peak values.
Simulation (LTspice): The values are calculated directly from their definitions using .meas or cursors. There is no noise and no bandwidth limit. 
However, the results depend on the time step and on the diode model, and RMS has to be taken over full periods after the transients have settled.

1.d) Several limitations can affect accuracy:
Sampling rate: If it is too low, short peaks can be missed or the signal can alias.
Bandwidth: The higher harmonics of square and pulse waves are attenuated, so the edges and peaks become rounded.
Waveform type: Average-responding meters give wrong results for non-sinusoidal waves, and signals with a high crest factor can exceed the meter’s limits.
Coupling: AC coupling removes the DC component and can distort low-frequency signals.
Measurement window: RMS calculated over a non-integer number of periods gives an error.
Noise: Spikes affect the peak and Vpp readings more than the RMS value.
Scope resolution: The scope has only 8 bits, so the signal should fill the screen.
Probes: The probe setting (×1 or ×10) must be correct. Voltages across components that are not grounded need the two-probe differential method, which adds some error.
Simulation: The time step size and the accuracy of the model affect the results.

1.e) For Vp and Vpp, the oscilloscope with DC coupling is the most appropriate method. These are instantaneous values, so we need to see the waveform, and a multimeter cannot measure them. 
Our signals are around 100 Hz, so bandwidth and sampling rate are not a problem. DC coupling keeps the real peak values of the asymmetric clipper and rectifier outputs. We would check the automatic measurements with cursors, and use CH1 − CH2 for components that are not grounded.
For Vrms, We would use the oscilloscope’s cycle RMS measurement, or a true-RMS multimeter, which is fine at 100 Hz. The waveforms in this lab (triangular, clipped, rectified) are not sine waves, so an average-responding meter would give wrong values. If the multimeter is in AC mode, we would also measure the DC value and calculate the total RMS as √(V_DC² + V_ACrms²).
